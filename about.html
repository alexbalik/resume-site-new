About

Some History and Personality:

My background starts without an interest in math or physics. In high school, I wasn't aware of my underlying passion for science. For this reason, I spent most of my time with an undeclared major in college, taking heavy coursework in the liberal arts like history and philosophy. In particular, writing philosophy papers was satisfying to me because of the orderliness, and necessity to clearly define words before using them. At the same time, it felt like something was missing - I didn't feel confident that you could really make sense of things with just words.

Later on, a couple of courses away from graduating with a business degree, I took an Astronomy course that changed the way I felt about science. I found myself daydreaming about Astronomy topics in business classes, imagining neutrinos flying through the Earth and being captured in distilled water chambers. After talking with my professor, I found that the closest major to Astronomy offered at my school was Physics, and decided to take a summer of accelerated physics and calculus classes. These classes were torturous at first, but solving these deeper technical problems fulfilled my desire to make sense of the world with more than just the English language. From there, I switched my major to Physics and never looked back.

I think my unusual path to science positions me not just for technical work, but also for technical leadership and people management. In my exploration, I've developed an ability to talk about complicated topics in precise but simple ways. These skills help me get to the root of problems fast, and get others on board when solving hard problems. In general, I also tend to spark lots of short, high energy conversations with others to extract important information. This attitude towards problem solving and communication has followed me into my professional career and is a key part of my process for doing good work.

Professional Career:

After doing pen and paper physics in my undergraduate days, I felt strongly about using the skills I learned to do meaningful work for the world. In this sense, I started at a company with a mission focused on preventing electrical house fires. It wasn’t exactly my passion, but I felt that I woke up in the morning with real purpose. At the same time, I wanted to land a more purely technical role, which is why I ended up at my current company where I write lots of code and help develop products for space and defense.

In general, I felt that building computational skills would help bring my understanding of physics to life. In this sense, I taught myself a few different programming languages along with object-oriented programming. Now, my current focus is to continue refining my developer skills through traditional "software 1.0" programming where I write every line of code, while also giving myself space to write code with natural language using Cursor, Claude Terminal, and agents in general. Even in a world where developers think less about syntax, I want to have the foundation to think critically about constraints I give to LLMs for code generation.
